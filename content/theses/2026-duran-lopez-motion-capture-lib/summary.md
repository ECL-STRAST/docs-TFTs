Quantitative analysis of human movement plays a fundamental role in multiple areas of biomedical engineering, particularly in clinical evaluation, rehabilitation, and the study of motor disorders. Accurate kinematic data allow clinicians and researchers to objectively evaluate functional limitations, monitor patient progress, and design personalized therapeutic interventions. Traditionally, this type of analysis has relied on specialized motion capture laboratories equipped with optical systems, force platforms, and dedicated biomechanical instrumentation. Although these systems provide high precision, their high cost, limited accessibility, and constrained environments significantly restrict their widespread clinical adoption.

In recent years, extended reality (XR) technologies have emerged as a promising alternative for capturing and analyzing human motion outside traditional laboratory settings. This opens new opportunities for biomedical applications such as home-based rehabilitation and the assessment of patient behavior in controlled, yet flexible virtual environments.

However, despite the growing interest in XR-based motion analysis, several challenges remain before these technologies can be reliably adopted in biomedical contexts. From an engineering perspective, XR systems are primarily designed for interactive rendering and user experience rather than for precise and temporally stable data acquisition. Moreover, most XR applications lack standardized mechanisms for persistent data storage and structured export, limiting reproducibility and post-processing capabilities.

Within this context, the present work is framed as part of the LibreMotion project, whose objective is to democratize the capture, storage, and analysis of biomechanical data using XR technologies. Specifically, this thesis focuses on the design and development of LibreMotion Capture, a cross-platform software library that aims to capture head and hand motion data from virtual reality (VR, a type of XR) applications and export them to formats compatible with conventional biomedical workflows. Thus, providing a robust and extensible software infrastructure that bridges interactive VR environments with established biomechanical standards, enabling the use of VR as a viable sensing platform for movement analysis.
